# My Thoughts on AI
February 26, 2026

As AI creeps ever further into our work and personal lives I couldn’t help but think of The Legend of John Henry, a struggle of “man against machine.” The legend depicts a railroad worker by the same name who, while working to connect Tidewater Virginia with the Ohio River Valley through the Big Bend Mountain in West Virginia, went up against a steam drill to prove that a man could work better and faster than the drill could (see [The Legend of John Henry: Talcott, WV](https://www.nps.gov/neri/planyourvisit/the-legend-of-john-henry-talcott-wv.htm) to read more). John Henry beat the drill but quickly died of exhaustion. It’s ironic given the fact that John Henry dies while fighting the very thing that was meant to save him. I began to wonder, why did John Henry choose to fight the machine? The legend is meant to inspire and symbolize those who gave their lives to work in dangerous conditions but would John Henry have fought the machine if he knew that it would become the standard regardless of whether he lived or died? 

John Henry could have learned to drive the machine. Sure his original job would have become obsolete but the task of using the drill, something that John Henry would have probably been quite good at, presented itself as a new opportunity. Despite that, John Henry was stubborn and paid the ultimate price. I am starting to see similar themes in my profession and in my day-to-day life. There are some people that embrace AI and others, like John Henry, that want nothing more than to fight it.

I am by no means saying that AI will fight us back or even kill us. I am simply stating that there are lots of people who want to pretend that AI isn’t real or that AI can’t take their jobs or their meaning. Speaking generally, these people have probably tied their purpose, their soul, their one reason for being on this planet to something that can now be taken away. Some might call that a fate worse than death while others might rejoice and see a plethora of untapped opportunity. Personally, I see both sides. I understand why AI has become so incredibly unavoidable and even use it daily. But a part of me wishes that AI was never invented. When speaking with others we often reminisce about the times before AI - when you had to actually Google things.

Jokes aside I don’t like how quickly things get done when AI is involved. Not because I think AI is wrong, it’s actually quite good at most things now, but because I didn’t get to struggle through the task and get better at something. I worry that I will become lazy. It’s human nature for most of us to take the path of least resistance. There are some outliers of course but most people are lazy. The roots of AI have already taken hold at home, in the workplace, and at school. Like a drug, every time you use AI you become more and more dependent on it. Think about it, how often do you find yourself presented with a task that should take a few hours. A few years ago you would have thought nothing of that task or the time it would take you to complete it. Today you are presented with a choice: complete the task manually or give it to your AI assistant who can complete that same task in just a few seconds. Before you ask, no I purposefully didn’t use AI to write this article, it is entirely thought of and written by me, but it took me hours.

Modern companies are not helping either. They are hell-bent on being the "first" or the "best" at something having to do with AI. Fortunately, it’s not super common to be fired from your job due to AI at the time I am writing this article. I believe that is because most companies don’t yet know how to fully harness AI. There have also been many instances of companies rejecting AI because they thought they would get more of a return on their initial investment. I personally don’t think that current AI systems have the capability to operate with a skillset that would put an entire, human dominated, industry out of permanent work but the CEOs of those companies really want to think that. The scary part to me is that I think we are pretty close to that reality. However, I believe a few things need to happen first.

For starters, AI is non-deterministic which means that an AI system can produce different outputs from the same input. In other words, if you asked an AI system to do something subjective, like create a modern website, you will very likely get a different website every time you ask even if your prompt is exactly the same. In an enterprise environment this non-determinism complicates testing, debugging, and quality assurance. All things that a company would need to guarantee its customers if they hope to turn a long-term profit. So in order for AI to actually replace jobs in the long run it would need to have more predictable and repeatable outputs.

Second, out of the box an AI model is not very useful. A model needs access to “tools" in order to understand the problem it is trying to solve or to actually solve the problem. For instance, an AI model needs access to Github if you asked it to make a change to a codebase or to Jira if you requested it to update a work ticket. These “tools” are API connections that can be insecure, configured improperly or otherwise dangerous to use. For example, an AI model could decide to re-write all the code in your codebase even if you simply asked it to update the README file. This problem can be partially solved with a “human in the loop”, or someone that verifies that the actions of the AI system were correct. However, that is a reactive measure and can lead to more time wasted in the review process than if a human did everything in the first place.

Third, an AI system needs to know how to actually do the thing you want it to do. This problem is starting to be solved with the use of “skills” or reusable instructions that the AI model can reference when crunching through a task. It’s not a perfect solution and in my experience I have had to remind the AI system I was talking to that it had certain skills it thought it was missing. In an enterprise environment I would expect the AI system to know exactly how to do something I asked it to do without any of my help (after all, I am talking to a computer). If you put yourself into the perspective of a user who isn’t AI literate you might not know what skill to tell the AI system about or, better yet, know how to question the output of said AI system to make sure the answer it gives you is correct. This would lead to more confusion than acceleration in my opinion.

Fourth, AI systems operate on something called a “context Window” which is the total amount of text (measured in tokens) that the model can consider at one time. This essentially functions as the model’s working memory during a prompt. Some models have a larger context window than others and many models have sufficient working memory to take on most tasks. However, the more information, tools, and skills you feed an AI system as part of your prompt, the less context you have left for continuing a conversation or asking a follow-up question. If an AI system is going to be fully replacing humans in any jobs it will need to gain an infinite context window that can be used for long durations.

To conclude, I am excited to see how quickly AI can develop over the next six to eight months. However, I am cautiously optimistic to see how business leaders leverage those developments. I worry that hasty adoption of a promising new technology will bring negative consequences. In the meantime, I will continue to do my part to learn about, rather than fight, the machines.
